# Twelve Days: The Story of the 1956 Hungarian Revolution

---

- **Publisher:** Pantheon Books, New York [6] (Originally published by Weidenfeld & Nicolson, London [6])
- **Publication Year:** 2006 [6]
- **ISBN-10:** 0-375-42458-x / **ISBN-13:** 978-0375424588 [5, 6]
- **Genre:** Non-fiction Historical Narrative / Cold War History [5]

---

## 2. Core Synopsis & Context
*Twelve Days* provides a fast-paced, authoritative, and front-page style account of the 1956 Hungarian Revolution, marking its 50th anniversary [5]. Grounded in newly released Soviet and Hungarian official documents, eyewitness testimony, and personal diaries, Sebestyen traces the dramatic twelve days of the uprising [5]. 

The narrative spans from the armed streets of Budapest—where citizens used petrol bombs and rifles to resist Soviet armor—to high-level power-brokering in Moscow and Washington [5, 17]. It captures the brief window when the uprising nearly succeeded, the subsequent brutal Soviet backlash (Operation Whirlwind), and the relative inaction of Western powers who had previously encouraged rebellion [5, 233, 307].

---

## 3. Structural Book Outline
The book is organized into three primary chronological divisions [7]:
1. **Part One: Prelude (pp. 5–104):** Examines the post-WWII Soviet occupation of Hungary, starting with the 1944 Moscow "percentages deal" [24], the brutal imposition of Stalinism under [[matyas-rakosi]] and the AV0 [16, 29, 33], the rise and fall of [[imre-nagy]]'s "New Course" [73, 77-80], and the mounting social tensions through the summer of 1956 [91, 102].
2. **Part Two: Revolution (pp. 105–272):** Chronologically details the daily events of the uprising from Tuesday, October 23, to Sunday, November 4, 1956, tracking street battles, the toppling of the Stalin statue [118], key military defense actions at the Kilian Barracks [130, 144], political shifts within the Nagy Cabinet [163, 185], and the Suez Crisis distraction [177, 208-209].
3. **Part Three: Aftermath (pp. 273–304):** Covers the violent pacification of Hungary, the massive refugee exodus [280], the abduction and eventual secret execution of Imre Nagy and other revolutionary figures under [[janos-kadar]]'s regime [285, 290-292].
4. **Appendix: The Sixteen Points (pp. 303–304):** The foundational student-written manifesto of the revolution [120, 315].

## 4. Key Historical Actors & Profiles
To maintain an atomic vault structure, these actors are wikilinked to their respective subject notes in the `01-history/02-reference/` or `01-history/02-evergreen/` folders [3].

### Hungarian Leaders & Dissidents
- **[[imre-nagy]] (1896–1958):** A reform-minded Communist "Land Divider" who implemented post-war land reform [22, 39]. He served as Prime Minister (1953–1955) [14], was purged by Rakosi, and was swept back to power by popular demand on October 24, 1956 [125, 133]. Nagy reluctantly embraced the revolution's radical demands, including declaring Hungary's neutrality and leaving the Warsaw Pact [234, 240]. He was abducted, tried in secret, and executed in 1958 [14, 285, 292].
- **[[janos-kadar]] (1912–1989):** An underground Communist organizer who suffered horrific torture and imprisonment during the Rakosi purges [13, 142-143]. He initially supported Nagy's revolutionary government and declared a "national communism" [13, 213]. However, on November 1, he defected to the Soviets [13, 241-242]. Installed by Moscow as Hungary’s puppet leader, he orchestrated the execution of Nagy to secure his own authority [13, 290-291].
- **[[pal-maleter]] (1917–1958):** A highly decorated former Horthyite officer who joined the anti-Nazi partisans [131]. Sent by the regime to restore order at the Kilian Barracks, he instead joined the insurgents, becoming a celebrated military leader of the revolution and Nagy's Defence Minister [130-131, 145, 187, 254]. He was arrested by KGB Chief Ivan Serov during mock withdrawal negotiations and was later hanged [14, 261, 292].
- **[[matyas-rakosi]] (1892–1971):** The brutal dictator of post-war Hungary and self-proclaimed "Stalin’s best pupil" [16, 27]. He orchestrated the systematic destruction of democratic opposition via "salami tactics" [25] and presided over a reign of terror that prosecuted over 1.3 million citizens [41]. Purged by Moscow in July 1956 due to rising loathing [92-94].
- **[[cardinal-jozsef-mindszenty]] (1892–1975):** Prince Primate of the Hungarian Catholic Church [34, 35]. A fierce opponent of Communism, he was tortured and sentenced to life imprisonment in 1949 [35, 36, 39]. Liberated during the revolution on October 31 [14, 205], he broadcast appeals to the nation [258] and eventually spent 15 years in sanctuary at the US Legation [14, 266].

### Soviet & International Power-Brokers
- **[[yuri-andropov]] (1914–1984):** The calculating Soviet Ambassador to Hungary [77, 179]. He tracked rising tensions and brilliantly misled Nagy's government, using mock negotiations to buy time for the Soviet military build-up while orchestrating the trap to arrest Maleter and kidnap Nagy [244, 248-249, 261, 285].
- **[[nikita-hrushchev]] (1894–1971):** First Secretary of the Soviet Communist Party [64]. His de-Stalinization "secret speech" destabilized the satellite states [84, 99]. Despite initial hesitation and support for political compromise, he authorized Operation Whirlwind to violently reassert Soviet imperial control [176, 216, 233, 264].
- **[[dwight-eisenhower]] & [[john-foster-dulles]]:** US President and Secretary of State who campaigned heavily on "rollback" and the "liberation of captive peoples" [17, 54, 56]. In practice, they pursued a cautious policy of contain-and-wait, prioritizing domestic elections and the Suez Crisis over actual military or diplomatic intervention on behalf of the Hungarian revolutionaries [17, 88, 139, 157, 180, 208].

## 5. Secondary Concepts & Vault Handoffs
Following the Taxonomic Directive [3], complex conceptual frameworks extracted from *Twelve Days* should be organized into independent Evergreen/Reference notes, categorized under the Subject `hungarian-revolution` (or `cold-war-conflict`) [1, 2]:

- **[[salami-tactics]]:** The step-by-step political strategy deployed by Rakosi to fragment and eliminate bourgeois, center-right, and social democratic opposition parties between 1945 and 1948 [25, 43].
- **[[the-16-points-manifesto]]:** The list of demands compiled spontaneously by students at the Budapest Technological University on October 22, 1956, defining the ideological aims of the uprising [102, 120, 315].
- **[[rollback-rhetoric-vs-containment]]:** An analysis of the contradiction in US foreign policy under the Eisenhower administration, which funded aggressive anti-Communist propaganda via Radio Free Europe but avoided intervention to preserve the European status quo [17, 55, 75, 103, 180].
- **[[suez-crisis-geopolitics]]:** Explores how the simultaneous Anglo-French military action in Egypt distracted Western leaders and provided a convenient political smoke screen for the Soviet military crack-down in Budapest [17, 177, 208-209, 217, 251].
