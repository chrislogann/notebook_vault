### The Birth of Kansas

#### Overview
An analytical examination of the political, economic, and factional struggles surrounding the establishment of the Kansas state government from 1859 to 1863 [6]. The text deconstructs the idealized narrative of the state’s founding to expose how personal aggrandizement, railroad speculation, and severe factional schisms between conservative and radical wings shaped early Kansas institutions [6, 12, 22].

#### Core Concepts

*  **The Robinson-Lane Factional Schism:** Early Kansas political history is defined by a bitter, personal, and systemic schism within the Free-State party (which eventually organized as the Kansas Republican Party) [9, 12, 13]. This division was personified by the incompatible temperaments of its two leaders: Governor Charles Robinson—who was calm, deliberate, legalistic, and shrewdly ambitious in business—and General James H. Lane—a temperamental, highly active, and opportunist radical who preferred direct armed action over diplomacy [9]. This schism manifested in major local disputes, including Lane's murder of Gaius Jenkins in 1858 over a disputed land claim [12], conflicting railroad routes through Lawrence (with Lane advocating for the south side of the river and Robinson for the north side) [12, 13, 24], and opposing stances on "Jayhawking" vigilantes (such as James Montgomery and John Brown), whom Lane defended and Robinson condemned as revolutionary [10, 11, 12].

*  **The Osawatomie Convention & Party Realignment (1859):** By 1859, the practical settlement of the free-soil question left the old Free-State party "worn out" [15]. Lane and Champion Vaughan organized a new political vehicle to secure federal patronage by affiliating with the National Republican Party [11, 13]. At the Osawatomie Convention (May 18, 1859), attended by Horace Greeley, the party adopted a platform based on the Declaration of Independence, free-soil in the territories, a liberal homestead act, and the construction of a Pacific railroad [14, 16]. This consolidation effectively dissolved the old Free-State party, while the Democrats organized a pro-popular-sovereignty counter-alignment at Tecumseh [17, 18].

*  **The Wyandotte Constitutional Convention & The "Capital Plot":** While the Wyandotte Constitution used the Ohio Constitution of 1851 as a model [44, 65], it was deeply compromised by a corrupt apportionment trade [33, 61]. An "enrolling clerk error" in the convention bill privately signed by the presiding officers and the Governor completely disfranchised Wyandotte County (a Democratic stronghold) by deleting its 2 delegates, while secretly adding 3 delegates to Leavenworth and 1 to Doniphan [32, 33, 40, 42]. These extra delegates from Leavenworth and Doniphan subsequently cast the deciding votes to locate the temporary capital at Topeka [60, 61]. This "Capital Plot" involved extensive bribery, pooling of votes, and lobbying by Topeka promoters [60, 61, 62], culminating in Topeka's city council secretly appropriating $19,500 in municipal funds to secure the permanent capital in the 1861 popular election [119].

*  **The Famine of 1860-1861 & The Political Exploitation of Relief:** A devastating drought in 1859–1860 plunged the territory into a severe famine [22, 27]. S. C. Pomeroy (Atchison) and Thaddeus Hyatt managed the Kansas Territorial Relief Committee [23, 25], but Pomeroy heavily politicized the relief work, using it to "ride into the United States Senate on the famine horse" [23, 25]. Relief supplies were disproportionately diverted to politically pivotal counties to secure votes (Allen and Linn received 107 lbs/capita, while Wyandotte and Douglas received only 38 and 6, respectively) [26]. Pomeroy also practiced "double entry" bookkeeping (paying teamsters in clothing but recording it as cash) and utilized unexplained funds to build an Atchison city hall, maintain a luxurious $2,000 senatorial residence in Topeka, and run a private pony express to "operate upon the consciences of the legislators" during the 1861 Senatorial election [24, 25, 26, 27].

*  **The Pacific Railroad Act & Indian Land Speculation:** Political and economic fortunes were tied directly to railroad speculation and the Pacific Railroad Act of 1862 [21, 22, 111]. Under the Delaware (1860) and Pottawatomie (1861) treaties, the Leavenworth, Pawnee, and Western (L., P., & W.) Railroad bought vast tracts of unreserved tribal lands cheaply at $1.25/acre [113, 114]. These treaties were accompanied by massive distributions of land "favors" (thousands of acres) to influential political insiders, including Ewing, Robinson, Usher, and Pomeroy [113, 114]. To secure the passage of these lucrative treaties in the U.S. Senate, Lane forced his rivals Robinson and Stevens to resign from the L., P., & W. directory, replacing them with his own political lieutenants [114].

*  **The Robinson Administration, Border Anarchy, & The Impeachment of 1862:** Entering office in February 1861, Governor Robinson faced a state treasury depleted by famine and completely lacking in armaments [94, 137]. The outbreak of the Civil War triggered severe border anarchy, with both Confederate raiders and Union "Jayhawkers" plundering the population [138, 139, 140, 144]. Robinson was powerless to suppress this violence because the factionalized legislature refused to appropriate funds to arm or equip the state militia [145, 156]. Seizing upon a cash-starved treasury, Robert S. Stevens negotiated the fraudulent sale of $95,600 of 7% State bonds to the Secretary of the Interior (Caleb B. Smith) at 85 cents on the dollar, but only accounted 60 cents to the State [163-170]. This "Bond Swindle" was seized upon by Lane's faction to impeach Robinson, J. W. Robinson (Secretary of State), and George S. Hillyer (Auditor) in 1862 [175, 177]. After the Senate was heavily "purged" of Robinson supporters by ousting senators holding military commissions [179, 180], the court convicted and removed the Secretary and Auditor, but acquitted Governor Robinson due to a lack of direct complicity [186-188].
